RECEPTIVE LANGUAGE FINDINGS

The child identified familiar objects and several common actions in both languages. Performance was stronger for concrete nouns than for relational words. Some vocabulary appeared in only one language, which was expected given differences in experience. Credit across languages improved the descriptive picture of his knowledge.

Difficulties remained after the clinician accounted for distributed vocabulary. The child frequently confused directions involving location, quantity, and order. He sometimes completed the final action while omitting the first. Spanish presentation improved selected items but did not eliminate the pattern.

For a direction involving a cup and a bear, he moved the correct object but placed it beside the bear instead of beneath it. Demonstration produced an accurate response. On a new item, the same relationship again required support. This suggested incomplete understanding rather than simple unfamiliarity with one English word.

Yes-or-no answers were reliable for highly familiar preferences. Questions about events required more support. He answered concrete who questions more successfully than why questions. The clinician did not expect mature explanations, but even simple event questions often produced an unrelated label.

Gestures and repetition improved access to instructions. However, repeated wording without visual support produced smaller gains. Adults should check understanding through the child's action rather than through a request to repeat the direction.

The combined evidence indicates receptive difficulty across languages, especially when meaning depends on relationships between words rather than recognition of individual labels.

EXPRESSIVE LANGUAGE AND LANGUAGE SAMPLE

[[CHILD|Mateo]] communicated requests, protests, comments, and attempts to gain attention. His strongest spontaneous messages involved visible objects and familiar routines. He used pointing, showing, and facial expression effectively. Spoken messages became less clear when the listener needed information about an absent object or earlier event.

The English sample contained frequent short combinations. Articles, auxiliary verbs, and location phrases were inconsistently included. The Spanish sample also showed limited sentence expansion and inconsistent marking of grammatical relationships. Cross-language differences were considered before identifying a form as concerning.

Examples included [[KEEP|"him go there"]], [[KEEP|"doggie eat"]], and [[KEEP|"yo querer ese"]]. These productions were recorded as spoken. They were not treated as personal identifiers. An English utterance transcribed as [[KEEP|"the bus the[EW] go"]] illustrated a repeated word during formulation.

His sister's name is [[SIBLING|Sol]]. After the clinician showed the picture and clarified the activity, he used [[KEEP|"sol"]] for the pictured target. The family member's name and the lexical target therefore require different annotation labels.

Teaching trials improved sentence length and inclusion of an action word. Generalization remained limited when the picture, verb, or communication partner changed. The child needed [[KEEP|mod cues]] during initial learning and [[KEEP|min cues]] during some repeated trials.

Narrative tasks revealed difficulty linking events. He named characters and objects but rarely expressed a clear sequence independently. Parent reports described the same limitation during explanations of classroom events.

SPEECH SOUND PRODUCTION AND INTELLIGIBILITY

Speech included several developmentally common simplifications, along with inconsistent reductions that affected unfamiliar listeners. The clinician considered English and Spanish sound systems separately. An accent or a sound pattern expected from language transfer was not counted automatically as a disorder.

The child sometimes substituted [[KEEP|[w]]] for English [[KEEP|/r/]] and omitted a consonant within longer words. Production of [[KEEP|/k/]] varied by word and task. He produced the target [[KEEP|"key"]] accurately after [[KEEP|min cues]] but used [[KEEP|"tea"]] in a later naming trial. Spanish targets showed stronger production of some shared consonants.

Connected speech was harder to understand than isolated words. Familiar context, gestures, and slower pacing improved listener understanding. His mother estimated that unfamiliar adults often requested repetition. This estimate was treated as parent report rather than a measured intelligibility score.

The speech profile warrants monitoring and selected treatment targets. The current evaluation did not establish a separate definitive speech sound diagnosis. Reduced message length and omitted grammatical information also contributed to communication breakdowns.

Voice quality, loudness, and resonance appeared adequate during the sample. No persistent stuttering pattern was observed. Occasional pauses occurred during language formulation. Oral movement findings did not suggest that strengthening exercises were indicated for the observed communication needs.

FUNCTIONAL COMMUNICATION AND PARTICIPATION

The primary functional concern is the child's ability to communicate enough information for another person to act. Requests often succeeded when the desired object was visible. Requests for help, explanations of problems, and descriptions of earlier events required substantial adult interpretation.

During pretend cooking, he handed over a closed container and said [[KEEP|"open"]]. This was effective. When a toy figure could not fit inside a bus, he repeated [[KEEP|"bus"]] without describing the problem. A model such as [[KEEP|"help, he is stuck"]] supported a more useful message.

With classmates, brief language can limit negotiation about materials and turns. Teacher notes described occasions when the child took an item after an unsuccessful verbal request. Staff interpreted these events within the communication context and used visual choices and short models.

The family already provides useful support. Caregivers pause, follow the child's interests, and interpret gestures. Some adults also ask several questions in quick succession. Reducing question load may allow more independent language.

The child demonstrated social interest and communicative intent throughout the session. His difficulties involve understanding and formulating language needed for participation. These findings support direct intervention combined with changes in everyday communication demands.

IMPRESSIONS

Findings support a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder, coded [[KEEP|F80.2]]. Evidence includes difficulty understanding relational directions, producing complete messages, and retaining newly taught structures across English and Spanish. Parent and teacher reports align with direct observations.

Bilingual exposure does not adequately explain the full pattern. The child showed distributed vocabulary and language-specific strengths, as expected. However, difficulties persisted within familiar routines and in the language used most often with each partner. Supported teaching improved performance but did not produce consistent independent transfer.

The English standardized language score contributes limited supporting evidence. The diagnosis rests on the combined history, bilingual sampling, functional observations, and learning trials. Historical developmental findings add context but do not independently establish the current diagnosis.

Speech sound production also affects clarity. Further sampling during treatment should clarify the persistence and functional effect of specific patterns. The clinician will avoid counting expected Spanish-English transfer as impairment.

Strengths include social engagement, meaningful gesture use, interest in shared play, responsiveness to visual support, and an involved family. These strengths provide a useful foundation for treatment.

RECOMMENDATIONS AND INITIAL PLAN

1. Begin speech-language therapy under CPT [[KEEP|92507]] twice weekly for an initial [[KEEP|12-week]] treatment block, subject to scheduling and coverage. Use play, books, and daily routines. Review progress before continuing the same frequency.

2. Address receptive and expressive language in both English and Spanish. Select words and structures that support the family's actual routines. Encourage caregivers to use the language in which they communicate most comfortably.

3. Target two-step directions containing familiar actions and one relational concept. An initial objective is [[KEEP|8/10]] correct responses, or [[KEEP|80%]], with [[KEEP|min cues]] across separate sessions. Reduce support gradually and test new materials.

4. Target complete requests, comments, and explanations. Teach a useful action word with needed information about the person, object, or location. An initial objective is [[KEEP|80%]] successful messages with [[KEEP|mod cues]], followed by increased independence.

5. Include communication repair. Teach the child to point, show, add a word, or use [[KEEP|"help me"]], [[KEEP|"ayudame"]], and [[KEEP|"I mean this"]]. Accept an effective repair before requesting a longer sentence.

6. Monitor speech patterns during meaningful language activities. Practice selected targets only when they improve functional clarity. Track performance for [[KEEP|/k/]] across words and conversation without treating every cross-language difference as an error.

7. Use short directions and allow processing time. Demonstrate unfamiliar relationships. Ask the child to act on the direction to check understanding. Avoid several consecutive questions when he is already struggling.

8. Coordinate classroom supports with Ms. [[TEACHER|Mara Lopez]]. Provide visual choices, predictable transition language, and opportunities for supported peer play. The parents may request a school evaluation to determine educational service eligibility.
