RECEPTIVE AND EXPRESSIVE LANGUAGE FINDINGS

Receptively, he identified familiar objects and actions in both languages. He understood common routines and simple questions about visible objects. Performance declined when directions included sequence terms, location contrasts, or several attributes. Repetition helped, but repeating the same sentence alone was less effective than pairing it with a visual demonstration.

During teaching, he improved his response to two-step directions from [[KEEP|5/10]] to [[KEEP|8/10]] with [[KEEP|mod cues]]. He needed continued support when the materials changed. This pattern suggests useful learning potential with incomplete transfer after brief practice.

Expressively, he used short sentences to request, comment, and protest. Longer explanations frequently omitted subjects, grammatical markers, or information needed by an unfamiliar listener. Spanish and English samples both showed difficulty organizing events. Some differences between the languages reflected expected bilingual development and were not counted as disorder features.

He used code switching to maintain conversation. The examiner accepted this behavior as a communication resource. Word-finding difficulty was identified when he could not retrieve a familiar concept in either language and relied on a general label or gesture.

During picture descriptions, [[CHILD|Nicolás]] produced [[KEEP|"him go there"]] and [[KEEP|"se cayó aquí"]]. The second production was appropriate for the immediate scene. The first lacked information about the person and destination. After a model, he expanded the message but needed another prompt to use the same structure with a different picture.

He answered concrete questions more easily than questions about causes or future events. Sentence models improved accuracy without eliminating the need for support.

CLINICAL IMPRESSIONS

Findings support mixed receptive-expressive language disorder and speech sound disorder. The language concern involves comprehension of detailed messages, sentence formulation, and organization of explanations. The speech concern involves patterns that reduce intelligibility across languages.

The diagnostic impression is based on converging evidence. Standardized English scores support the description but do not carry the conclusion alone. Bilingual exposure, code switching, and accent features are expected parts of his language experience.

Strengths include social engagement, purposeful communication, imitation, family involvement, and response to structured teaching. These strengths should support intervention. Progress will depend on consistent opportunities to practice and careful adjustment of support.

There was no clear evidence during this visit of a primary voice, fluency, or social communication disorder. This assessment does not establish conclusions about cognitive ability, educational eligibility, or broader developmental diagnoses.

RECOMMENDATIONS

1. Begin bilingual speech-language treatment twice weekly. Address comprehension, sentence formulation, narrative organization, and speech clarity through meaningful play and daily routines.

2. Practice following two-step directions with pictures and objects. Reduce support gradually. An initial goal is [[KEEP|8/10]] correct responses with [[KEEP|min cues]] across different materials.

3. Teach sentence forms that identify the actor, action, and essential detail. Accept Spanish, English, or mixed responses when the task measures message content.

4. Practice explanations with short picture sequences. Help the child state what happened first, what changed, and how the event ended.

5. Target [[KEEP|/k/]] and [[KEEP|/g/]] across both languages. Select functional words with the family. Move from supported syllables to words and connected speech as accuracy improves.

6. Use brief home practice during existing routines. Model an expanded sentence after the child speaks. Allow time for a response and avoid requiring repeated correction during every conversation.

7. Continue both home languages. Family members should use the language that supports natural, detailed interaction. There is no recommendation to replace Spanish with English.

8. Share classroom supports with Ms. [[TEACHER|Alina Duarte]] after caregiver authorization. Useful supports include visual directions, advance vocabulary teaching, and time to repair unclear messages.
