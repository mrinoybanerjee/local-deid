Receptively, [[CHILD|Emilio]] identified familiar objects, actions, and basic body parts in either language. He understood routine directions such as [[KEEP|“Put the car in the box”]] and [[KEEP|“Dame el perro”]] when the relevant materials were visible. Performance declined when directions included two actions, an unfamiliar verb, or a location contrast. On unsupported trials, he often completed the final action only. Repetition helped somewhat, while pictures and a demonstration produced a larger improvement. This pattern suggests a need to reduce processing demands while teaching language explicitly.

Understanding of questions varied by context. He answered concrete [[KEEP|“what”]] and [[KEEP|“qué”]] questions more successfully than [[KEEP|“why”]] and [[KEEP|“por qué”]] questions. When asked where a character had gone, he sometimes named the character rather than a location. He identified the target word [[KEEP|“under”]] inconsistently and responded more reliably to [[KEEP|“debajo”]] during play. Esa diferencia was treated as an exposure-related possibility, not proof that one language was globally stronger. Similar difficulty appeared across languages when questions required inference.

Expressively, he communicated through gestures, single words, short phrases, and occasional longer sentences. In a mixed-language sample containing [[KEEP|50 utterances]], most messages served to request, label, protest, or direct play. Productions included [[KEEP|“Doggie se cayó”]], [[KEEP|“Yo want ese”]], and [[KEEP|“Está sleeping aquí”]]. These switches were communicatively appropriate. The concern was limited information and inconsistent sentence structure across both languages. Several messages omitted the person or action needed for a listener to understand the event without seeing it.

**Impresiones y recomendaciones**

Findings support a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder, ICD-10 [[KEEP|F80.2]], based on converging caregiver concerns, classroom reports, bilingual assessment, and observed functional limitations. Difficulties affected understanding of complex messages, sentence formulation, vocabulary access, and connected descriptions across languages. Bilingual exposure and code-switching do not explain the full pattern. Standardized scores contribute to this impression but are not the sole basis. His engagement, symbolic play, family participation, and response to supported teaching are strengths that can support progress.

Recommend individual speech-language treatment using CPT [[KEEP|92507]], initially once weekly for [[KEEP|45 minutes]], with caregiver participation and periodic classroom collaboration. Treatment should support both languages according to communication partners and routines. Early goals should address following related two-step directions, producing sentences with clear agents and actions, and explaining short event sequences. Begin with pictures and models, then reduce support. A practical initial criterion is [[KEEP|80%]] accuracy across varied activities with [[KEEP|min cues]], followed by checks of independent use.

At home, adults should describe actions during everyday routines and pause for a response. If [[CHILD|Emilio]] says [[KEEP|“Dog there”]], an adult can model [[KEEP|“The dog is under the table”]] or [[KEEP|“El perro está debajo de la mesa”]]. Respond to meaning first, then add language. Avoid requiring repeated correction before honoring a clear request. Shared book reading can include pointing, acting, and predicting. Short practice periods during enjoyable routines are preferable to lengthy drills that increase frustration.

At preschool, provide directions in small parts and pair new vocabulary with pictures or demonstrations. Ask him to show or explain the first step before adding another. During group discussion, allow extra wait time and offer a visual choice when needed. Adults should invite classmates to wait while he completes his message. Peer play can include turn-taking roles that give him a clear reason to speak. Classroom staff should record examples of independent communication, including successful Spanish responses when an adult can understand them.

Maintain hearing follow-up according to the pediatric and audiology plans, particularly after further ear infections. Review progress after [[KEEP|12 weeks]] of treatment using caregiver examples, classroom information, and fresh language samples. A review is provisionally planned for [[DATE|June 2, 2025]]. If speech clarity continues to limit participation, complete a focused bilingual speech sound assessment. Coordinate educational eligibility decisions with the school team; this clinical report supplies information but does not independently determine school services.
