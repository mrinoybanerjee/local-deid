RECEPTIVE LANGUAGE

The child identified familiar objects more consistently in Spanish. Errors increased when directions included two actions, a location word, or an unfamiliar verb. He sometimes completed the final action while omitting the first. This pattern appeared in both languages, including routines caregivers described as familiar.

Pictures, slower presentation, and demonstration improved performance. During a supported task, he completed [[KEEP|8/10]] directions, or [[KEEP|80%]], with [[KEEP|mod cues]]. Because support changed the task demands, this result was not treated as independent mastery.

He understood basic object functions during play but had difficulty answering questions about where an item belonged. Yes-or-no responses were inconsistent when questions contradicted the visible scene. The clinician therefore interpreted some answers as response habits rather than clear evidence of comprehension.

EXPRESSIVE LANGUAGE

Spontaneous communication included gestures, single words, and short combinations. Spanish phrases occurred more often during pretend cooking. English phrases occurred more often with school-related materials. The child produced [[KEEP|"más jugo"]], [[KEEP|"car go"]], and [[KEEP|"mamá aquí"]]. He expanded a request after a model but rarely added detail without support.

A picture of the sun elicited the target word [[KEEP|"sol"]]. This target is an ordinary Spanish word and also matches his sister’s name, [[SIBLING|Sol]]. When shown a family photograph, he used [[KEEP|"Sol aquí"]] while pointing to his sister, [[SIBLING|Sol]]. These separate contexts were recorded to distinguish a vocabulary target from a person reference.

A bilingual language sample contained short utterances with limited verb variety. The clinician used [[KEEP|[EW]]] for an extraneous word where the sample required that notation. Mixed-language utterances were retained without marking language mixing as impairment.

The child had difficulty describing a completed event. With pictures, he named participants and actions but needed prompts to connect them. Caregiver examples suggested similar difficulty recounting activities at home.

SPEECH, VOICE, FLUENCY, AND SOCIAL COMMUNICATION

The speech sample showed variable production of [[KEEP|/k/]] and [[KEEP|/g/]], with occasional fronting in English words. The target [[KEEP|"key"]] was produced as [[KEEP|"tea"]] during one trial. Some English liquid productions included [[KEEP|[w]]]. These patterns require interpretation within age, language, and word context.

Connected speech was less clear than isolated words. Familiar listeners understood messages more readily when the topic was visible. The child improved [[KEEP|/k/]] production with [[KEEP|mod cues]], suggesting that placement support may help during treatment.

Voice quality and loudness appeared functional during the session. No consistent stuttering-like behavior was observed. The child used eye gaze, shared enjoyment, and gestures to coordinate play. He responded to another person’s interests and sought help when materials were inaccessible. Communication breakdowns sometimes led to withdrawal, but he returned when the listener offered a clear choice.

IMPRESSIONS

The combined findings support a provisional clinical impression of mixed receptive-expressive language disorder, [[KEEP|F80.2]]. Difficulties affected comprehension of familiar directions, sentence expansion, and event description in both languages. These concerns were also reported across home and school settings.

The English vocabulary score alone cannot explain the full communication profile. Stronger Spanish vocabulary demonstrates the importance of language exposure. However, difficulties with familiar Spanish directions and connected expression suggest needs beyond limited English experience.

Speech findings indicate reduced clarity that warrants monitoring and targeted support. The current sample does not establish a separate speech diagnosis with confidence. Additional connected speech samples in both languages will help clarify consistency and functional impact.

Strengths include social interest, purposeful gestures, shared play, responsiveness to visual support, and family participation. The child’s response to models supports a trial of direct intervention. Prognosis depends on attendance, appropriate language access, practice within routines, and continued review of progress.

RECOMMENDATIONS AND SERVICE SCHEDULE

1. Begin bilingual speech-language treatment focused on functional comprehension and expression. Use [[KEEP|92507]] for the proposed individual treatment service, subject to the fictional authorization terms.

2. Continue Spanish at home. Caregivers should use the language that supports natural interaction and clear explanations. Accept meaningful responses in either language.

3. Practice short directions within familiar routines. Begin with visible objects and one action. Add a second action after the child succeeds consistently.

4. Expand the child’s message by adding one useful element. For example, respond to [[KEEP|"car go"]] with [[KEEP|"the car goes fast"]]. Allow time for a response without requiring repetition.

5. Support communication repair through pointing, showing, and simple choices. Give the child an opportunity to clarify before a sibling answers.

6. Collect progress data in both languages. Record independence separately from [[KEEP|min cues]] and [[KEEP|mod cues]]. Review whether gains transfer to home and school.
