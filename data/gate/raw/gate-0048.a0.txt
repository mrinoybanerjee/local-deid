The [[KEEP|Preschool Language Scales, Fifth Edition, Spanish]] was administered using its bilingual procedures. The [[KEEP|PLS-5 Spanish]] provided the standardized language scores reported below.

Conversational samples were examined using [[KEEP|Systematic Analysis of Language Transcripts]] conventions. Relevant [[KEEP|SALT]] annotations included [[KEEP|[EW]]] for selected word-level errors and [[KEEP|[EU]]] for utterance-level errors.

5.2 Participation and validity

Attention varied during longer verbal tasks. The findings are considered a reasonable estimate of performance that day, with bilingual experience and caregiver information incorporated into interpretation. They do not establish a fixed limit on future learning.

6.2 Receptive language

[[CHILD|Mateo]] identified familiar objects, actions, and common body parts. He understood routine requests in either language when the situation provided clear clues. Spanish responses were more consistent for household vocabulary; English responses were stronger for several classroom routines.

Difficulty increased with unfamiliar spatial concepts, longer directions, and questions requiring relationships between events. For example, he located an item placed on a table but needed demonstration when asked to position it behind another object.

During teaching, accuracy improved with gesture, contrastive examples, and [[KEEP|mod cues]]. He retained several newly taught words during a delayed play activity. This response supports the use of structured teaching while leaving open the need to measure learning across repeated sessions.

6.3 Expressive language

Spontaneous messages usually contained brief combinations of words. Longer messages occurred during familiar pretend play but often omitted information needed by an unfamiliar listener. Vocabulary was distributed across languages rather than contained entirely within either language.

Observed productions included [[KEEP|"yo quiero carro"]], [[KEEP|"he go casa"]], and [[KEEP|"más big one"]]. These examples show code-switching and developing sentence structure. Code-switching itself was not scored as an error.

Separate concerns included limited elaboration, inconsistent verb marking within each language, and difficulty forming complete explanations. When prompted about a classroom event, he named objects but did not independently describe the sequence. With picture support and [[KEEP|mod cues]], he added an action and a location.

7. SPEECH SOUND AND ORAL MECHANISM FINDINGS / HABLA

7.1 Speech production

Speech sampling showed reduced accuracy for velar sounds, consonant clusters, and some word-final consonants. The target [[KEEP|/k/]] was sometimes produced as [[KEEP|[t]]], particularly in longer words. The target [[KEEP|/g/]] showed a similar forward placement pattern.

During imitation, the target [[KEEP|"casa"]] was produced as [[KEEP|"tasa"]]. The English target [[KEEP|"school"]] was produced as [[KEEP|"cool"]]. A glide substitution, [[KEEP|[w]]], occurred in some English words containing [[KEEP|/ɹ/]]. That finding was interpreted within developmental and bilingual context.

Patterns were examined across both languages. Differences attributable to Spanish phonology were distinguished from patterns that reduced clarity in both languages. The combined effect of several patterns, message length, and reduced repair skills limited conversational intelligibility.

7.2 Stimulability and oral observations

[[CHILD|Mateo]] produced [[KEEP|/k/]] in isolation with [[KEEP|max cues]] and achieved [[KEEP|6/10]] accurate attempts after practice. Accuracy decreased in syllables and connected speech.

Facial symmetry, lip movement, and tongue movement appeared adequate for the observed tasks. No structural concern was identified through this limited examination. Diadochokinetic tasks were not interpreted because participation was inconsistent. Voice and fluency did not present a primary concern during the sample.

8. SOCIAL COMMUNICATION AND PLAY / COMUNICACIÓN SOCIAL

8.1 Interaction

[[CHILD|Mateo]] initiated shared play, showed toys to adults, and checked listeners' reactions. He used pointing and facial expression effectively. When misunderstood, he sometimes repeated the same phrase louder rather than changing the message.

His teacher described successful play with classmates [[PEER|Mila Rentería]] and [[PEER|Owen Calder]]. Communication was harder when classmate [[PEER|Tessa Mora]] changed the game rules quickly. These observations suggest that language demands affected participation.

8.2 Narrative and repair

A picture sequence about a lost toy elicited labels and isolated actions. He needed direct support to identify the problem and explain the ending. After modeling, he used [[KEEP|"first"]], [[KEEP|"then"]], and [[KEEP|"al final"]], with [[KEEP|mod cues]].

During pretend veterinary play involving [[PET|Canela]], he sustained interaction longer and offered more spontaneous language. This familiar theme may provide a useful starting point for treatment.

9. IMPRESSIONS / IMPRESIÓN CLÍNICA

9.1 Clinical interpretation

Findings indicate receptive and expressive language weaknesses affecting participation across Spanish and English. Standardized results, functional observations, caregiver concerns, and teacher reports support a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder. The proposed diagnostic code is [[KEEP|ICD-10 F80.2]].

Speech sound patterns also reduced intelligibility beyond the effects of language choice alone. The proposed speech sound diagnosis is [[KEEP|ICD-10 F80.0]]. Conclusions should be reviewed as treatment provides additional samples.

Bilingual exposure does not explain all observed difficulties. At the same time, this evaluation does not support reducing Spanish use. Family language remains central to relationships, learning, and communication.

9.2 Strengths and limitations

Strengths include social interest, pretend play, gesture use, and improvement with teaching. Limitations include a single assessment session, variable attention, and incomplete early medical records. Classroom observation would help confirm how these findings affect daily routines.

10. RECOMMENDATIONS / RECOMENDACIONES

10.1 Intervention plan

1. Begin bilingual speech-language treatment targeting comprehension, sentence expansion, speech clarity, and communication repair.
2. Use Spanish and English according to the activity, family priorities, and the child's available vocabulary.
3. Pair spoken directions with pictures or gestures, then reduce support as accuracy improves.
4. Practice selected speech targets in meaningful words before increasing phrase length.
5. Teach repair strategies through modeling, choices, and supported practice.
6. Coordinate classroom supports with Ms. [[TEACHER|Nadia Flores]] and the family.

10.2 Initial functional targets

Treatment should address following two-part directions, describing actions with sufficient detail, and answering simple questions about recent events. Suggested criteria include [[KEEP|8/10]] successful opportunities across repeated sessions, with support reduced toward [[KEEP|min cues]].

Speech practice should begin with stimulable targets, including [[KEEP|/k/]], and monitor generalization across languages. Targets should reflect functional words rather than accent modification.

10.3 Home practice and follow-up

Caregivers can expand a short message by adding one useful detail, then allow time for a response. Durante la lectura, pause and invite a comment instead of asking continuous questions. Accept mixed-language responses.
