[[CHILD|Mateo]] was [[KEEP|2;8]] at testing. Birth history reviewed with the family: born at [[KEEP|38 weeks]] gestation, birth weight [[KEEP|6 lb 14 oz]], Apgar scores of [[KEEP|8]] and [[KEEP|9]], delivered at [[HOSPITAL|Piedmont Regional Hospital]].

On the [[KEEP|Preschool Language Scale-5 (PLS-5)]], administered in both English and Spanish per bilingual protocol, [[CHILD|Mateo]] earned an Auditory Comprehension standard score of [[KEEP|92]] ([[KEEP|30th percentile]]) and an Expressive Communication standard score of [[KEEP|68]] ([[KEEP|2nd percentile]]), with an age equivalent in the [[KEEP|14-16 month]] range for expressive skills. On the [[KEEP|Rossetti Infant-Toddler Language Scale]], parent report and direct observation together placed his expressive vocabulary at approximately [[KEEP|25]] words across both languages, well below the [[KEEP|50-100 word]] range expected near his age. His receptive language was judged age-appropriate, which is an important and reassuring finding: [[CHILD|Mateo]] understands both English and Spanish well; his difficulty is specifically with expressive output, not comprehension, and not simply bilingual exposure.

Articulation screening showed emerging but inconsistent sounds. [[CHILD|Mateo]] produced his own name as "Teo" and attempted the word "perro" as "[[KEEP|eo]]," omitting the initial [[KEEP|/p/]] and final [[KEEP|/o/]] distortion. During structured imitation, he produced the target sound [[KEEP|/k/]] correctly in [[KEEP|8 of 10]] ([[KEEP|80%]]) trials with [[KEEP|moderate cues]], and attempted the final consonant in "more" (English) as [[KEEP|[w]]] for /r/ with [[KEEP|minimal cues]]. These results, together with his limited word combinations and family-reported frustration, meet criteria for a diagnosis of [[KEEP|F80.2]] (mixed receptive-expressive language disorder, predominantly expressive in presentation at this time). We explained to the family that this diagnosis reflects a true language delay and is separate from, and not caused by, growing up hearing two languages — bilingualism itself does not cause or worsen a language disorder.
