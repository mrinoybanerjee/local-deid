[[CHILD|Mateo]] showed difficulty with several speech sounds that are typically mastered by his age, including [[KEEP|/s/]], [[KEEP|/z/]], [[KEEP|/ɾ/]] (the Spanish tapped r), and consonant clusters like [[KEEP|/st/]] and [[KEEP|/bl/]]. He tends to simplify clusters (saying "[[KEEP|tar]]" for "star") and sometimes substitutes [[KEEP|[w]]] for [[KEEP|/ɾ/]] in Spanish words, which is a very common pattern for bilingual children his age and is not, by itself, concerning.

During articulation testing, we used a set of target words that included common nouns chosen for their sounds, such as "sapo," "silla," "luna," and "sol." When shown a picture of the sun and asked to name it, [[CHILD|Mateo]] looked at the card, smiled, and said "[[KEEP|sol]]," producing it clearly with only a mild distortion on the final consonant. He then giggled and added, in Spanish, "¡como mi hermana!" — like my sister — referring, of course, to his sister [[SIBLING|Sol]]. We thought this moment was worth including here, not because it affected the score (it did not; "[[KEEP|sol]]" was scored simply as the target word for the /s/ and /l/ sounds), but because it shows how aware [[CHILD|Mateo]] is of the connection between words and the people he loves, which speaks to his strong social and pragmatic skills.

In a separate drilling activity, [[CHILD|Mateo]] was asked to imitate the word "[[KEEP|sol]]" five times in a row with [[KEEP|mod cues]] (a visual cue card plus a verbal model). He achieved [[KEEP|4 of 5]] correct productions, which is a strong response to cueing and a good prognostic sign for therapy.

In our Spanish language sample, collected during a short play-based conversation with [[PROVIDER|Rosa Delgado]] leading and me observing, [[CHILD|Mateo]] used sentences of [[KEEP|3-4 words]] on average, with vocabulary appropriate for his age, including family-specific words like "abuela," "perro," and the name of his dog, "[[PET|Max]]." He used Spanish grammar markers correctly most of the time, though he occasionally mixed English words into Spanish sentences — for example, saying "quiero el truck" instead of "quiero el camión." This kind of code-mixing is developmentally typical and expected in bilingual children and is not itself a sign of a language disorder.

Impresiones / Impressions

Based on standardized testing, parent and teacher report, and our clinical observations, [[CHILD|Mateo]] presents with a speech sound disorder affecting several consonants and consonant clusters in both English and Spanish, alongside a mild-to-moderate expressive language delay in English. This diagnostic picture fits the ICD-10 code [[KEEP|F80.1]] (Expressive Language Disorder) along with [[KEEP|F80.0]] (Phonological Disorder). Given that [[CHILD|Mateo]] is being raised bilingually, with ongoing, rich exposure to both Spanish and English, we do not believe that bilingualism itself is a cause of his difficulties; rather, he appears to show a true speech and language disorder present across both of his languages, which is the pattern we would expect to see if an English-only child presented similarly.

[[CHILD|Mateo]] qualifies for speech-language therapy services based on standard scores falling [[KEEP|1.5]] standard deviations or more below the mean on both the [[KEEP|PLS-5]] Expressive Communication subtest and the [[KEEP|GFTA-3]].
