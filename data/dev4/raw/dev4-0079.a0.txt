Speech Sound Production

On the [[KEEP|GFTA-3]], administered in English, [[CHILD|Pepe]] obtained a standard score of [[KEEP|65]] ([[KEEP|1st]] percentile), indicating a significant speech sound disorder relative to English monolingual peers. Error patterns included final consonant deletion, stopping of fricatives, and gliding of liquids. Specific sound errors included substitution of [[KEEP|/t/]] for [[KEEP|/k/]] in word-initial position (e.g., "[[KEEP|[t]up]]" for "cup"), substitution of [[KEEP|[w]]] for [[KEEP|/ɾ/]] and [[KEEP|/l/]], and frequent final consonant deletion (e.g., "[[KEEP|ca_]]" for "cat"). On the Spanish phonological screening, [[CHILD|Pepe]] demonstrated some of these same patterns, notably gliding of [[KEEP|/ɾ/]] (e.g., producing "[[KEEP|[w]opa]]" for "ropa"), suggesting that his speech sound errors are not solely attributable to typical second-language phonological transfer and are present across both of his languages.

Speech intelligibility in connected speech was estimated at approximately [[KEEP|60%]] for unfamiliar listeners and approximately [[KEEP|80%]] for familiar listeners, consistent with parent report.

Oral Mechanism Examination

Oral mechanism examination revealed structures and function within normal limits for speech production. No signs of dysarthria or significant oral-motor weakness were observed. Diadochokinetic rates were judged to be mildly slow for his age but adequate to support intelligible speech with intervention.

Language Sample Analysis

A conversational language sample was collected during unstructured play and analyzed using [[KEEP|SALT]]. [[CHILD|Pepe]]'s mean length of utterance in morphemes (MLU-m) was [[KEEP|3.1]] in English and [[KEEP|3.6]] in Spanish, both below expected ranges for his age in each language. Omission of grammatical morphemes was noted in English, including frequent omission of the copula "is" (e.g., "[[KEEP|[EW]]] he happy" for "he is happy") and inconsistent use of plural -s and past tense -ed. In Spanish, [[CHILD|Pepe]] demonstrated inconsistent gender agreement on articles and adjectives (e.g., "la perro" instead of "el perro") and omission of some verb inflections.

During the sample, when asked to imitate the target phrase "the dog runs," [[CHILD|Pepe]] produced "[[KEEP|dog wun]]," omitting the article and substituting [[KEEP|[w]]] for [[KEEP|/ɾ/]]. When shown a picture of his own dog and asked what the dog was doing, he spontaneously said "[[PET|Rocky]] wun," again substituting [[KEEP|[w]]] for the target [[KEEP|/ɾ/]] but correctly using the pet's name rather than "dog."

Narrative and Pragmatic Skills

[[CHILD|Pepe]] was asked to retell a simple picture-sequence story in both languages. His narratives in both languages were brief, consisting primarily of single-clause utterances with minimal use of connectives or temporal markers. He relied heavily on pointing and gesture to supplement verbal output, particularly when a target word was unavailable to him in the language being used. Pragmatically, [[CHILD|Pepe]] demonstrated appropriate eye contact, turn-taking, and joint attention during structured and unstructured interaction, with no concerns regarding social communication noted at this time.

Trial Therapy / Stimulability Probes

Stimulability probes were conducted for several error sounds. With moderate cues (mod cues), [[CHILD|Pepe]] was able to produce [[KEEP|/k/]] correctly in isolation [[KEEP|8/10]] trials and in word-initial position [[KEEP|6/10]] trials. With minimal cues (min cues), he produced final consonants correctly in single words [[KEEP|3 of 5]] trials. With maximum cues (max cues) and visual modeling, he approximated [[KEEP|/ɾ/]] in Spanish in [[KEEP|2/10]] trials, suggesting this sound is not yet stimulable and will require a developmental hierarchy of cueing to establish.

IMPRESSIONS

Based on parent report, classroom observation, and standardized and criterion-referenced testing in both English and Spanish, [[CHILD|Pepe]] presents with a speech sound disorder and a language disorder affecting both his home language (Spanish) and his second language (English). Because speech and language weaknesses are present across both languages he has been exposed to since infancy, these findings are consistent with a primary speech sound disorder and developmental language disorder rather than patterns expected from typical second-language acquisition or limited English exposure alone.

[[CHILD|Pepe]] meets criteria for a diagnosis of Childhood Apraxia of Speech features were ruled out based on consistent, predictable error patterns rather than inconsistent groping or prosodic disruption; his profile is instead most consistent with a phonological speech sound disorder. Diagnostic impression: Speech Sound Disorder, consistent with ICD-10 code [[KEEP|F80.0]], and Mixed Receptive-Expressive Language Disorder, consistent with ICD-10 code [[KEEP|F80.2]]. These delays are significant enough to interfere with [[CHILD|Pepe]]'s participation in preschool activities, peer interaction, and family communication, and are expected to affect early literacy development without intervention.

[[CHILD|Pepe]]'s stronger performance in Spanish relative to English on several measures supports continued language exposure in Spanish at home and underscores the importance of delivering intervention bilingually rather than shifting the family toward English-only strategies. His family's strong engagement, as evidenced by consistent attendance at [[AGENCY|Piedmont CDSA]] appointments and willingness to participate in home practice, is a significant strength supporting positive outcomes.
