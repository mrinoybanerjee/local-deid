SPEECH SOUND PRODUCTION

On the GFTA-3 (English administration), [[CHILD|Mateo]] produced a Standard Score of 58, falling well below average and consistent with a severe phonological/articulation delay in English. Error patterns observed included frequent final consonant deletion (e.g., "ca" for "cat," "do" for "dog"), stopping of fricatives (e.g., "tun" for "sun"), and gliding of liquids (e.g., "wed" for "red"). Stimulability testing showed [[CHILD|Mateo]] could approximate /s/, /r/, and final consonants with moderate cueing (mod cues), suggesting the errors are developmental and stimulable rather than fixed.

Informal Spanish articulation screening using the SAM items revealed similar error patterns transferring across languages, with notable difficulty producing word-final /s/ and /n/, frequent cluster reduction (e.g., "pato" for "plato"), and inconsistent production of the tap/trill distinction between /r/ and /rr/. During a targeted word-level task designed to probe sibilant and liquid production in Spanish, this examiner elicited the target word "sol" (meaning "sun" in Spanish) as part of a standard single-word list; [[CHILD|Mateo]] produced [[KEEP|"to"]] for the target [[KEEP|"sol"]], substituting a stop for the fricative and deleting the final liquid, consistent with his broader error pattern. It is noted for the record that this target word is homophonous with the name of [[CHILD|Mateo]]'s younger sister, [[SIBLING|Sol]], though no name-specific association was evident in his error pattern; this appeared to be a coincidental lexical overlap rather than an avoidance behavior, and his production of her name in spontaneous speech during the session (calling for her when she cried in the waiting room) showed the identical substitution pattern, [[KEEP|"to"]] for [[KEEP|"Sol"]].

Additional single-word productions elicited and transcribed using IPA included: [[KEEP|"casa"]] produced as [[KEEP|[kata]]], [[KEEP|"perro"]] produced as [[KEEP|[peo]]], and [[KEEP|"flor"]] produced as [[KEEP|[fo]]]. In English, [[CHILD|Mateo]] produced [[KEEP|"school"]] as [[KEEP|[ku]]] and [[KEEP|"spoon"]] as [[KEEP|[pun]]]. Intelligibility in connected speech was estimated at approximately [[KEEP|3/10]] for unfamiliar listeners and [[KEEP|6/10]] for familiar listeners (parents, examiner by end of session), consistent with mother's report that "solo yo y su papa le entendemos casi todo."

LANGUAGE SAMPLE AND EXPRESSIVE/RECEPTIVE SKILLS

A 50-utterance spontaneous language sample was collected in Spanish during free play with toy animals (including a stuffed dog resembling the family pet [[PET|Canela]], which [[CHILD|Mateo]] spontaneously referenced several times, saying "[[KEEP|ese es como mi perro]]"). Mean length of utterance in morphemes (MLU-m) was calculated at 3.1, which falls below expected range for his chronological age of 4;2 (expected range approximately 4.5-5.5 for Spanish-speaking children this age). SALT coding identified frequent omission of articles and object pronouns (e.g., omitting "lo" in "quiero lo" produced simply as [[KEEP|"quiero"]]) and inconsistent verb conjugation, with overgeneralization of present tense forms across past and future contexts [SALT code: [[KEEP|[EW]]] used for several word-finding errors].

An English language sample of 50 utterances, collected during structured play with this examiner, showed even more limited output, with MLU-m of 1.8 and heavy reliance on single-word utterances, gestures, and code-switched insertions of Spanish nouns into otherwise English utterance attempts (e.g., "I want agua," "mas please"). This pattern of code-switching is developmentally appropriate for a bilingual child and should not, in isolation, be considered evidence of disorder; however, the overall reduced MLU and limited grammatical complexity in both languages, combined with the significant articulation delay described above, indicates a true language impairment present across both of [[CHILD|Mateo]]'s languages rather than a language difference attributable solely to bilingual development.

Receptively, [[CHILD|Mateo]] followed one-step directions reliably in both languages and two-step directions inconsistently (approximately [[KEEP|6/10]] trials in Spanish, [[KEEP|4/10]] trials in English). He identified common objects and body parts accurately in Spanish (approximately [[KEEP|9/10]]) with somewhat reduced accuracy in English ([[KEEP|7/10]]). He demonstrated understanding of basic spatial concepts (in, on, under) in Spanish but inconsistent understanding of the same concepts when tested in English.

IMPRESSIONS / IMPRESIONES

Based on standardized testing, language sampling, parent and teacher report, and clinical observation, [[CHILD|Mateo]] presents with a Mixed Receptive-Expressive Language Disorder affecting both Spanish and English, co-occurring with a moderate-to-severe Speech Sound Disorder characterized by multiple phonological process errors (final consonant deletion, stopping, cluster reduction, gliding) that are present and consistent across both of his languages. His difficulties are not attributable to bilingual exposure alone; true language impairment affecting both Spanish and English, combined with reduced speech intelligibility, is significantly impacting his ability to communicate with family, peers, and teachers, and is contributing to observed behavioral frustration, including increased aggression toward his younger sister [[SIBLING|Sol]] and social withdrawal from peers at [[DAYCARE|Little Sprouts Bilingual Preschool]].

Given his medical history of recurrent otitis media and tube placement, ongoing monitoring of hearing status is also recommended, though current screening results are reassuring. [[CHILD|Mateo]]'s strong nonverbal communication skills, good imitation skills, stimulability for error sounds with moderate cueing, and strong family support (including an engaged mother, father, grandmother, and extended family) are significant strengths that are expected to support a positive response to intervention.

[[CHILD|Mateo]] meets eligibility criteria for speech-language intervention services under [[AGENCY|CDSA of the Triangle]] guidelines based on standard scores falling more than 1.5 standard deviations below the mean in multiple domains across both languages.
