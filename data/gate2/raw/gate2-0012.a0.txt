## 1. Reason for Referral and Evaluation Questions

### 1.1 Referral concerns

[[CHILD|Nicolás]] was referred for a bilingual speech-language evaluation because his family and preschool staff reported difficulty understanding directions, explaining needs, and producing clear sentences. His mother described frequent reliance on pointing when a listener did not understand him. His father reported that conversations often stopped after an initial request.

The referral requested information about communication across Spanish and English. It also requested recommendations for treatment and classroom support. The evaluation addressed whether the reported difficulties reflected differences in language exposure, an underlying communication disorder, or both.

### 1.2 Scope and sources

This assessment combined parent interviews, preschool observations supplied by staff, record review, structured tasks, and play-based sampling. Information about development came from caregivers unless a reviewed record is identified.

The clinician completed the evaluation in Spanish and English. No interpreter participated. Conclusions reflect the child's performance during this appointment and the information available at the time. The report does not establish an educational eligibility decision.

### 7.2 Receptive language

On [[KEEP|Auditory Comprehension]], the child identified familiar objects and actions with greater success than relational concepts. He understood routine requests when the relevant object was visible.

He followed unfamiliar directions containing a location contrast on [[KEEP|3 of 5]] opportunities with [[KEEP|mod cues]]. Performance decreased when the direction required retaining several pieces of information without visual support.

He showed emerging understanding of simple questions about actions. Questions about reasons, future events, and sequence required demonstrations or choices. Similar difficulty appeared in both languages, although familiar Spanish vocabulary improved access.

### 7.3 Expressive language

On [[KEEP|Expressive Communication]], the child used words for requests, labels, refusals, and comments. Spontaneous utterances usually contained short combinations. Sentence structure became less stable when he described an event beyond the immediate setting.

Observed productions included [[KEEP|"mamá mira carro"]], [[KEEP|"I want ese"]], and [[KEEP|"no cabe aquí"]]. These examples showed meaningful communication and flexible use of available vocabulary.

English productions sometimes omitted grammatical elements. Spanish productions also showed limited sentence expansion and inconsistent verb marking. The presence of difficulty across languages supported concern beyond differences in English exposure alone.

### 7.4 Dynamic learning

Before teaching, he expanded a basic action description on [[KEEP|2/10]] opportunities. After modeling and supported practice, performance increased to [[KEEP|7/10]] with [[KEEP|mod cues]].

He retained the practiced structure in a new play activity on [[KEEP|4/10]] opportunities with [[KEEP|min cues]]. This response suggests useful learning potential and continued need for guided practice.

## 8. Speech Sound Production, Oral Mechanism, and Voice

### 8.1 Speech sound observations

Selected [[KEEP|GFTA-3]] items were used to elicit English consonants. No standardized articulation score was calculated because bilingual considerations and administration changes limited normative interpretation.

The child produced several early consonants consistently. Errors included omissions in consonant clusters and variable production of [[KEEP|/k/]], [[KEEP|/g/]], and Spanish [[KEEP|/ɾ/]].

In connected speech, he sometimes used [[KEEP|[t]]] for [[KEEP|/k/]]. One elicited production was [[KEEP|"tat"]] for the target [[KEEP|"cat"]]. Spanish words also showed occasional syllable reduction.

A familiar caregiver understood approximately [[KEEP|85%]] of sampled utterances with context. The clinician understood approximately [[KEEP|65%]] without caregiver explanation. These estimates reflect this sample and listener context.

### 8.2 Stimulability and movement

The child imitated [[KEEP|/k/]] in isolation on [[KEEP|3/5]] attempts with [[KEEP|max cues]]. Accuracy decreased when the sound was placed in words.

Visible oral structures appeared adequate for the tasks completed. Lip closure, tongue movement, and jaw stability supported eating history and speech participation. The assessment did not identify a clear motor speech pattern, although further observation is warranted.

### 8.3 Voice and fluency

### 9.2 Areas of need

Conversation was strongest around visible objects. He needed help maintaining a topic when the listener asked an unfamiliar question. Language limitations appeared to restrict participation despite clear social interest.

The clinician recommends interpreting interaction alongside language demands. This evaluation alone does not resolve broader developmental questions. Persistent concerns across settings should be discussed with the pediatric provider.

## 10. Clinical Impressions

### 10.1 Language formulation

The available evidence supports a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder, ICD-10-CM [[KEEP|F80.2]]. Relevant findings include difficulty understanding unfamiliar directions, limited sentence expansion, and reduced ability to explain events across Spanish and English.

Bilingual exposure is part of the child's communication history. It does not adequately explain the full pattern of difficulties observed. This conclusion rests on converging functional evidence rather than the descriptive standardized values alone.

### 10.2 Speech formulation

Speech sound errors reduce clarity and support a speech sound disorder formulation, ICD-10-CM [[KEEP|F80.0]]. Treatment should distinguish developmentally expected patterns and language differences from errors that affect both languages or limit participation.

### 10.3 Prognosis and priorities

Prognosis for functional improvement is favorable with consistent intervention and caregiver participation. The child showed social motivation, responsiveness to models, and measurable improvement during supported learning.

The main priorities are understanding everyday language, producing useful sentences, repairing communication breakdowns, and improving speech clarity. Hearing follow-up remains an important supporting action.

## 11. Recommendations and Proposed Treatment Goals

### 11.1 Direct intervention

Recommend individual speech-language therapy twice weekly for an initial treatment block, subject to authorization and family scheduling. Treatment may be billed under CPT [[KEEP|92507]] with modifier [[KEEP|GN]].

The clinician should provide intervention in Spanish and English according to the child's needs and communication partners. Family members should continue using languages they speak comfortably.

Use focused stimulation, recasts, and play-based practice. Selected principles from [[KEEP|Hanen]] may support caregiver coaching. A [[KEEP|Cycles]] approach may be considered if repeated sampling confirms suitable speech sound patterns.

### 11.2 Proposed goals

1. Follow functional directions containing an action and a location concept on [[KEEP|8/10]] opportunities with [[KEEP|min cues]].
2. Produce an expanded sentence to request, describe, or comment on [[KEEP|8/10]] opportunities with [[KEEP|mod cues]], then reduce support.
3. Repair a communication breakdown by adding a word, gesture, or description on [[KEEP|4/5]] opportunities with [[KEEP|min cues]].
4. Produce selected speech targets in meaningful words on [[KEEP|80%]] of opportunities with [[KEEP|mod cues]].
5. Demonstrate carryover of a practiced communication strategy during a familiar routine on [[KEEP|3 of 5]] observed opportunities.

### 11.3 Home and preschool support

Adults should gain attention before speaking, use short meaningful directions, and pause for a response. Pair new vocabulary with objects, pictures, and actions. Expand the child's message without requiring repeated correction.

At [[DAYCARE|The Tumbleglass Lantern]], staff should preview unfamiliar activities and offer clear language models during peer play. Ms. [[TEACHER|Rosales]] can record brief examples of successful requests and communication breakdowns.

Practice should remain connected to daily activities. Caregivers can describe actions during meals, dressing, and shared books. They should accept appropriate responses in either language.

### 11.4 Follow-up

Recommend audiology follow-up if listening concerns or middle-ear symptoms continue. Share this report with the pediatric provider and the family-selected educational team.

A progress review is proposed for [[DATE|January 8, 2027]]. Repeat assessment should examine functional change and use standardized procedures only when administration conditions support valid interpretation.
