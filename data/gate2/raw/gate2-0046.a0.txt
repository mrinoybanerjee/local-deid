COMPRENSIÓN / RECEPTIVE LANGUAGE

[[CHILD|Emilio]] identified familiar objects and understood routine action words in both languages. He followed a familiar direction when the materials and adult’s gesture made the task clear. Understanding decreased when directions included a location concept, an unfamiliar adjective, or several actions without contextual support.

He identified pictured actions in [[KEEP|8/10]] opportunities, or [[KEEP|80%]], with [[KEEP|min cues]]. He followed novel two-part directions in [[KEEP|3 of 5]] opportunities with [[KEEP|mod cues]]. For location words, performance was [[KEEP|4/10]] independently and [[KEEP|7/10]] after a model. The targets included [[KEEP|“under”]], [[KEEP|“behind”]], [[KEEP|“debajo”]], and [[KEEP|“detrás”]].

He sometimes selected the most familiar object rather than following the full sentence. For example, when two animals differed by size and location, he attended to the animal name but missed the relationship. A shorter sentence and visual demonstration helped. This pattern also appeared during Spanish tasks, suggesting that English exposure alone did not explain the difficulty.

His mother’s use of familiar wording improved attention, but did not remove all errors. The clinician observed stronger understanding during meaningful routines than during isolated questions. This strength can support treatment, provided that practice gradually includes new materials and communication partners.

No single behavior was interpreted outside its interactional context.

IMPRESIONES / CLINICAL IMPRESSIONS

Findings support a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder, provisionally coded [[KEEP|F80.2]]. Difficulties affected understanding of novel directions, independent sentence expansion, and communication repair across Spanish and English. Caregiver reports, classroom observations, and structured findings converged. Bilingual development and language mixing did not adequately explain the full pattern.

[[CHILD|Emilio]] showed strengths in social interest, familiar vocabulary, imitation, and learning with visual support. He responded to brief teaching and participated longer when tasks had a clear purpose. These strengths support a favorable treatment response, although the rate of progress cannot be predicted from one visit.

Speech sound differences may add to communication difficulty. A separate speech sound diagnosis remains deferred pending a larger bilingual sample and monitoring. The clinician also recommends continued hearing follow-up when symptoms occur. The present evaluation does not establish a broader developmental diagnosis or replace a multidisciplinary assessment.
