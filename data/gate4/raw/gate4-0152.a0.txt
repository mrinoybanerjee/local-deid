Comprensión / Receptive language

The child understood familiar action words and routine requests in both languages. In a supported Spanish task, he selected named objects in [[KEEP|8/10]] opportunities, or [[KEEP|80%]], with [[KEEP|min cues]]. Accuracy decreased when the direction included an unfamiliar relationship or an additional action.

For related two-step directions, performance was [[KEEP|3 of 5]] with [[KEEP|mod cues]]. He often completed the last action heard. A gesture showing the order improved performance. This pattern suggests difficulty holding and organizing verbal information, especially when the message contains several parts.

He understood the target words “[[KEEP|arriba]]” and “[[KEEP|abajo]]” during movement play. Understanding of “[[KEEP|detrás]]” and “[[KEEP|entre]]” was inconsistent across objects and languages. The clinician varied materials to check whether responses reflected a learned routine.

Questions about visible actions were easier than questions requiring explanation. He answered “[[KEEP|what happened?]]” by naming an object but often omitted the action. When asked “[[KEEP|¿por qué?]],” he sometimes repeated the final word instead of giving a reason.

A brief teaching trial paired spoken directions with pictures. After practice, accuracy increased from [[KEEP|4/10]] to [[KEEP|8/10]] with [[KEEP|min cues]]. The improvement supports the use of visual support and repeated teaching. It does not erase the difficulty observed without support.

Expresión / Expressive language

Spontaneous speech included requests, labels, protests, and brief comments. Common productions included “[[KEEP|más agua]],” “[[KEEP|I want carro]],” and “[[KEEP|no cabe]]”. These messages were effective when the listener could see the context.

The child used both languages within sentences. Code-switching itself was not counted as an error. For example, “[[KEEP|the perro sleeping]]” communicated an agent and action. The relevant concern was limited sentence organization and difficulty adding information when the listener needed clarification.

In a picture sequence, he produced “[[KEEP|boy fall]]” and then “[[KEEP|agua aquí]]”. He identified important elements but did not independently explain their connection. With [[KEEP|mod cues]], he produced “[[KEEP|el niño se cayó]]”. He did not retain the full sentence after the picture changed.

The code [[KEEP|[EW]]] identified selected English grammatical errors within English clauses. Spanish grammar and mixed-language clauses required separate clinical review. The code [[KEEP|[SI]]] marked an unintelligible segment.

Narrative difficulty appeared in both languages. He needed [[KEEP|max cues]] to introduce the setting, describe the problem, and explain the outcome. Sentence length increased during familiar pretend play, but longer utterances sometimes became less clear.

Comunicación social y aprendizaje / Social communication

The child shared enjoyment, shifted gaze between people and objects, and used gestures to direct attention. He brought a toy to the clinician and waited for a response. Social interest was a relative strength.

Communication repair was less consistent. When the clinician misunderstood a request, he repeated the same message without adding information. With [[KEEP|mod cues]], he pointed to the relevant object or selected a picture. This strategy helped the interaction continue.

During shared play, he completed [[KEEP|6/8]] turn exchanges with [[KEEP|min cues]]. He needed more support when the partner changed the topic. The task did not test peer participation under ordinary classroom noise or competing demands.

The clinician used [[KEEP|mediated learning]] to model a short problem statement, wait, and invite a new attempt. He learned the target “[[KEEP|help me open]]” during a container activity. After a break, he used “[[KEEP|abre, please]]” independently. This was an appropriate bilingual request.

His learning response supports active intervention with meaningful repetition. It also supports measuring carryover across partners and settings. Performance with one adult during one visit should not be treated as proof of independent classroom use.

Impresiones clínicas / Clinical impressions

Findings support mixed receptive-expressive language disorder, ICD-10 [[KEEP|F80.2]]. The central difficulties involve understanding complex messages, organizing sentences, explaining events, and repairing breakdowns. These difficulties affect home routines and preschool participation.

English test results alone cannot separate language difference from disorder. The diagnosis rests on converging information: concerns in Spanish and English, caregiver examples, classroom observations, limited narrative organization, and substantial support needs during functional tasks.

Speech sound differences also reduce intelligibility. Current evidence supports monitoring and targeted treatment within the communication plan. The available sample does not justify a separate motor speech diagnosis. Additional sampling may clarify which sound patterns persist across languages.

Strengths include social interest, meaningful gestures, engagement in pretend play, and improvement after teaching. The child communicates best when the topic is visible and the adult gives time to respond. These strengths provide practical starting points for intervention.

Recomendaciones / Recommendations

1. Begin individual speech-language treatment twice weekly for thirty minutes. Review participation, response to teaching, and family priorities after an initial treatment block. Use CPT [[KEEP|92507]] with modifier [[KEEP|GN]] when applicable to the documented service.

2. Use [[KEEP|focused stimulation]], [[KEEP|expansions]], and [[KEEP|recasts]] during meaningful activities. The clinician should model language without requiring the child to repeat every sentence. Consider caregiver coaching through [[KEEP|Hanen It Takes Two to Talk]] when the format fits the family.

3. Continue Spanish at home. Hablen como les salga natural. Adults should describe actions, pause for a response, and add one useful detail to the child’s message. Rich interaction matters more than assigning each language a rigid schedule.

4. Provide pictures for classroom routines and common problems. Teach the child how to point to a picture while attempting a message. A full [[KEEP|PECS]] program is not recommended from this assessment alone.

5. Select speech targets after further bilingual sampling. A [[KEEP|Cycles]] approach may be considered if consistent phonological patterns emerge. [[KEEP|PROMPT]] is not indicated solely because a child has reduced intelligibility.

6. Coordinate with the preschool and receiving school. Share functional examples and cueing requirements. Ask staff to note whether skills occur independently, with [[KEEP|min cues]], or with [[KEEP|mod cues]]. Use the same definitions across settings.

7. Continue hearing follow-up if new listening concerns occur. Review treatment progress on [[DATE|January 14, 2027]]. The family may contact the clinician sooner if communication skills regress or daily participation changes substantially.
