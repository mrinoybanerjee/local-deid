RECEPTIVE AND EXPRESSIVE LANGUAGE

[[CHILD|Mateo]] understood familiar nouns, common actions, and routine requests in both languages. He located preferred materials and followed familiar cleanup directions. Performance decreased when directions combined an unfamiliar verb, a location term, and an additional action. Repetition alone helped less than a demonstration.

During English testing, he sometimes selected an object associated with the activity rather than the requested object. During Spanish play, he showed similar difficulty when the direction required attention to a changing relationship between objects. This cross-language pattern supports concern beyond limited English exposure.

Expressive communication included gestures, single words, and short combinations. The child requested, protested, greeted, and commented. He named familiar objects more readily than he explained how something happened. Verbs and descriptive words appeared less consistently than nouns.

Examples included [[KEEP|"me want big truck"]], [[KEEP|"no cabe"]], and [[KEEP|"put aqui"]]. The clinician treated language mixing as an expected bilingual behavior. Mixing alone did not indicate impairment. More relevant concerns included limited sentence flexibility and difficulty giving enough information for an unfamiliar listener.

When the dog appeared in a family photograph, he said "[[PET|Rocky]] run." During a pretend telephone activity, he said "[[PARENT|Lupita]] help me." These productions showed meaningful use of familiar names and a clear communicative purpose.

A brief narrative task used pictures depicting a spilled drink. The child identified the main objects but needed [[KEEP|max cues]] to explain the cause and sequence. With a model and picture supports, he produced [[KEEP|"boy spill juice"]]. He later recalled the action with [[KEEP|min cues]].

Dynamic teaching targeted a new action word and a simple sentence frame. Independent performance increased from [[KEEP|2/6]] to [[KEEP|5/6]] after modeling, repetition, and supported practice. This improvement indicates useful learning potential, while the need for structured support remains clinically relevant.

IMPRESSIONS

The findings support a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder and a speech sound disorder. These conclusions rest on converging observations across languages and settings, rather than English reference scores alone. Difficulty understanding unfamiliar language, building flexible sentences, and explaining events affected participation in both English and Spanish.

Bilingual development explains some differences in vocabulary distribution, language mixing, and sound production. It does not fully explain the reported communication limitations across familiar Spanish routines and English classroom activities. Continued monitoring should consider changes in exposure and opportunities to communicate.

Strengths include social interest, pretend play, family engagement, willingness to imitate, and improvement after teaching. The child used communication for several purposes and responded well to visual support. These strengths provide practical starting points for intervention.

The evaluation does not establish an intellectual disability, autism diagnosis, or motor speech diagnosis. No direct assessment of those conditions occurred. Additional referral should follow specific concerns, rather than assumptions based on language scores.

RECOMMENDATIONS AND INITIAL GOALS

Begin bilingual speech-language therapy twice weekly, with sessions lasting approximately thirty minutes. Reassess frequency after observing attendance, response, and carryover. Coordinate treatment with preschool staff and caregivers so the child practices useful communication throughout the week.

Goal [[KEEP|1]]: During play and daily routines, the child will follow directions containing an action and a spatial relationship with [[KEEP|80%]] accuracy across [[KEEP|3 of 4]] sampled activities, using [[KEEP|min cues]].

Goal [[KEEP|2]]: The child will produce flexible sentences to request, describe, or explain during [[KEEP|8/10]] opportunities, using [[KEEP|min cues]]. Accept appropriate responses in either language and record the language used.

Goal [[KEEP|3]]: The child will produce [[KEEP|/k/]] and [[KEEP|/g/]] in selected functional words with [[KEEP|80%]] accuracy across repeated probes, using [[KEEP|mod cues]] initially and reducing support as accuracy improves.

Use parent coaching informed by [[KEEP|Hanen]] principles to increase responsive interaction and balanced conversational turns. Consider a focused [[KEEP|Cycles]] approach for recurring speech patterns when treatment data support that structure. Select targets that matter to the family and can be practiced across languages.

Caregivers should describe ongoing activities, expand short messages, and allow time for a response. For example, expand [[KEEP|"big truck"]] to [[KEEP|"the big truck goes"]]. Avoid requiring repeated imitation during every exchange. Meaningful communication should remain the main purpose.

Provide classroom visuals for directions and event sequences. Teach repair options such as [[KEEP|"show me"]], [[KEEP|"say again"]], and [[KEEP|"I need help"]]. Adults should acknowledge the message before asking for clearer speech.

Maintain Spanish at home. Shared reading, songs, cooking, and pretend play can support vocabulary and sentence growth without requiring English-only interaction. Offer brief practice activities that fit the parents' work schedules.
