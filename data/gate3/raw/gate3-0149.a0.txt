**Lo que observamos — Understanding and expression**

[[CHILD|Mateo]] identified familiar objects, understood routine action words, and followed directions involving objects in view. He completed [[KEEP|8/10]] familiar directions with [[KEEP|min cues]]. Directions containing two related actions were harder. He completed [[KEEP|3 of 5]] after repetition and a gesture, with [[KEEP|mod cues]].

Questions about what an object does were easier than questions about why something happened. Picture choices helped him show knowledge that was difficult to express aloud. When I reduced the amount of language and paused, he often responded successfully. This suggests that adults should allow processing time before adding more words or another question.

In play, he requested, protested, commented, and called for attention. Examples included “[[KEEP|yo want más]],” “[[KEEP|no cabe aquí]],” and “[[KEEP|help me abrir]]”. These productions show useful communication and flexible use of both languages. He also pointed, looked toward adults, and brought objects over to share a problem.

The language sample contained [[KEEP|42]] usable utterances. Mean length of utterance was [[KEEP|2.4]] words in this descriptive sample. This measure included both languages and was not compared with a monolingual cutoff. He expanded a message after an adult model in [[KEEP|6/10]] opportunities with [[KEEP|mod cues]], especially when the message concerned something he wanted.

He had more difficulty explaining a short event without pictures. He might name the object but leave out who acted or what changed. Sentence models helped: an adult could describe the action, pause, and accept his response. Repeated demands to say a sentence exactly increased frustration and reduced his willingness to communicate.

**Habla y sonidos — Speech findings**

Familiar listeners understood more than unfamiliar listeners. During connected speech, estimated intelligibility was [[KEEP|62%]] for an unfamiliar listener in the room. This estimate reflects the sample and listening conditions, rather than a standardized score. Speech became less clear when sentences grew longer or when he spoke quickly during an exciting activity.

Observed patterns included fronting of [[KEEP|/k/]] and [[KEEP|/g/]], occasional final consonant omissions in English, and variable production of Spanish [[KEEP|/ɾ/]]. He produced [[KEEP|[w]]] in some English words containing [[KEEP|/r/]]. These findings need interpretation within each language because sound expectations and word structures differ between Spanish and English.

For the target “[[KEEP|car]],” he said “[[KEEP|tar]]”. For “[[KEEP|gato]],” he said “[[KEEP|dato]]”. He produced [[KEEP|/k/]] accurately in [[KEEP|4/8]] practice attempts with [[KEEP|max cues]]. Accuracy improved when we slowed the task and used a visual model. There was no consistent visible struggle to begin a sound during this visit.

During animal play, he said, “[[PET|Canelo]] run aquí.” Later, he called, “[[SIBLING|Lina]] mira esto.” These utterances combined a person or pet name with useful language. Oral structures appeared adequate for the observed speech tasks. A brief observation cannot rule out every motor speech concern, but the current pattern supports further phonological assessment and treatment.

**Impresiones — What the findings mean**

The combined findings support a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder and a speech sound disorder in this fictional case. Proposed diagnostic codes are [[KEEP|F80.2]] and [[KEEP|F80.0]]. Concerns appeared across languages and settings, including familiar Spanish routines. Bilingual exposure alone does not explain the full pattern of difficulty.

Strengths include social interest, meaningful gestures, pretend play, family involvement, and a positive response to models. These strengths provide a good starting point for therapy. The assessment does not establish a reading diagnosis or a broader developmental diagnosis. School and family observations should continue to guide decisions as demands change.

**Recomendaciones — Our next steps**

1. Begin speech-language therapy twice weekly, with each visit lasting about thirty minutes. Use both languages according to the activity and communication partner. Initial work should focus on useful messages, understanding directions, and clearer speech. Reconsider frequency after the first treatment block based on participation and progress.

2. Coordinate with Ms. [[TEACHER|Bellara]] and the school team. Helpful classroom supports include short directions, visual choices, demonstration, and time to respond. Adults can check understanding by asking him to show the next action. They should avoid treating silence as proof that he did not understand.

3. Use family coaching informed by [[KEEP|Hanen]]. Follow his interest, describe what he is doing, and expand one step beyond his message. If he says “[[KEEP|más jugo]],” an adult can model “[[KEEP|quiero más jugo]]” without requiring an exact repetition.

4. Start speech practice with functional words selected by the family. A [[KEEP|Cycles]] approach may help organize work on sound patterns. [[KEEP|PROMPT]] is not proposed at this time. Picture supports can supplement speech; a formal [[KEEP|PECS]] program is not currently planned. Review these choices as new evidence appears.

5. Use brief practice during enjoyable routines. Dinner preparation, bath play, and feeding the dog provide natural opportunities. Ask fewer testing questions and make more comments. When he struggles, offer “[[KEEP|ayuda]]” or “[[KEEP|help me]]” as a model, then respond to his attempt.
