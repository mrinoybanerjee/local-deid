RECEPTIVE AND EXPRESSIVE LANGUAGE

During familiar play, the child identified common objects and actions in both languages. He followed related directions more consistently when the objects remained visible. He completed [[KEEP|8/10]] familiar single-step directions independently and [[KEEP|3 of 5]] related two-step directions with [[KEEP|min cues]]. Performance decreased when instructions included an unfamiliar location word or required remembering an item that was no longer visible.

Spanish comprehension was stronger for household vocabulary, while English comprehension was stronger for classroom routines. Across both languages, the child had difficulty explaining relationships, answering questions about a short event sequence, and identifying what a character might do next. Repetition alone did not consistently resolve these difficulties. Pictures, demonstration, and shorter sentences improved access to the task and reduced frustration.

Spontaneous language included requests, protests, labels, comments, and brief pretend-play dialogue. The child produced “[[KEEP|I want big truck]]” and “[[KEEP|el bebé está dormido]]” during supported play. He sometimes omitted small grammatical words in English and had difficulty producing consistent verb forms in Spanish. The clinician considered these patterns within each language and did not count ordinary language mixing as an error.

A picture sequence prompted “[[KEEP|boy fall]]” followed by a gesture toward the ground. With [[KEEP|mod cues]], the child added “[[KEEP|he fall because wet]]”. The response showed emerging cause-and-effect language but limited independent detail. Naming performance improved when the examiner accepted either language. However, difficulty retrieving action words and organizing a complete message remained evident across both languages.

The clinician used [[KEEP|Systematic Analysis of Language Transcripts (SALT)]] conventions for a brief descriptive sample. Clear lexical errors received [[KEEP|[EW]]], and unintelligible segments received [[KEEP|[X]]]. The sample was too short and context-dependent for a normative comparison. It nevertheless showed frequent reliance on gesture when the child needed to repair a message or describe an event without visible objects.

SPEECH SOUND FINDINGS

Speech errors reduced clarity in both languages. Observed patterns included inconsistent production of [[KEEP|/k/]] and [[KEEP|/g/]], reduction of some consonant clusters, and variable marking of final consonants. The child used [[KEEP|[t]]] for [[KEEP|/k/]] in “[[KEEP|cat]]” during naming. He also produced “[[KEEP|poon]]” for “[[KEEP|spoon]]”. Some English differences reflected language influence and were not automatically classified as disordered.

In Spanish, the child produced several multisyllabic words with omitted sounds or syllables. His production of [[KEEP|/ɾ/]] varied by word and speaking rate. The clinician did not diagnose an isolated rhotic problem from these observations. The broader concern was reduced consistency and clarity across familiar words. Connected speech was less understandable than single-word naming, especially when he spoke quickly or provided little context.

The clinician understood approximately [[KEEP|70%]] of English connected speech and [[KEEP|75%]] of Spanish connected speech during supported play. These are contextual estimates, not standardized scores. The mother understood more of the sample and supplied several meanings that the clinician could not confirm independently. Voice and fluency appeared functional during the visit. The observed sample did not support a separate voice or fluency diagnosis.

DYNAMIC ASSESSMENT AND PARTICIPATION

Teaching trials used [[KEEP|focused stimulation]], [[KEEP|recasts]], and [[KEEP|visual supports]]. After a model, the child produced the target phrase “[[KEEP|help me open]]” in [[KEEP|6/8]] opportunities with [[KEEP|min cues]]. He later used “[[KEEP|ayúdame a abrir]]” during a different activity. Carryover was less consistent when the materials changed, suggesting that he benefited from teaching but still needed support to generalize a new form.

For speech practice, the clinician introduced [[KEEP|/k/]] through brief listening and imitation activities. Accuracy improved from [[KEEP|2/10]] independent attempts to [[KEEP|7/10]] attempts with [[KEEP|mod cues]]. The child accepted feedback and remained engaged when practice alternated with play. This response supports a trial of structured intervention. It does not establish a motor speech diagnosis or predict a fixed treatment duration.

The child shared enjoyment, sought adult attention, and used gesture effectively. He became quiet after repeated communication failures but returned to play when the examiner acknowledged his message. Parents identified successful repair as their highest priority. They want him to ask for help, describe a problem, and continue an interaction without relying on an adult to guess the intended message.

IMPRESSIONS

Findings support a mixed receptive-expressive language disorder and a speech sound disorder, represented by [[KEEP|F80.2]] and [[KEEP|F80.0]]. Difficulty appeared across languages and affected everyday participation. The conclusion rests on converging observations, rather than English test scores alone. Strengths include social interest, symbolic play, use of gesture, family engagement, and a positive response to direct teaching with meaningful materials.

Bilingual exposure does not adequately explain the full pattern observed. However, language experience affects vocabulary distribution and some speech productions. Treatment must distinguish those differences from errors that limit communication across languages. The current assessment does not establish a broader developmental diagnosis. Additional evaluation should follow specific concerns identified by the medical or educational team.
