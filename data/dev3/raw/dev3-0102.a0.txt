Assessment Procedures

Over two sessions, I completed a full bilingual speech-language evaluation with Teo. I conducted all testing and conversation myself in both English and Spanish, moving between languages the way Teo naturally does at home, since I am bilingual and trained in bilingual assessment practices. Procedures included a parent interview with both of you, a classroom observation at Tumbleweed Treehouse Preschool, an oral-motor exam, standardized language testing, an articulation assessment, and an analysis of a spontaneous language sample collected during play.

I administered the Preschool Language Scale, Fifth Edition, Spanish-English Bilingual Edition (PLS-5 Bilingual), which measures both receiving and expressing language in whichever language, or combination of languages, the child uses. I also administered the Goldman-Fristoe Test of Articulation, Third Edition (GFTA-3) to look closely at Teo's speech sound production, and I used the Bilingual English-Spanish Assessment (BESA) Phonology subtest to compare his sound patterns across both languages. Speech Sound Production

During the GFTA-3 and BESA Phonology subtests, Teo showed consistent, patterned errors across both English and Spanish rather than inconsistent, random ones, which is an important distinction in bilingual children. He substitutes /t/ for /k/ in word-initial position in both languages, for example saying "tat" for the target word "cat" and "taza" sounding close to correct for the Spanish word "taza." He also deletes final consonants, so the word "dog" came out as "da," and he simplifies consonant clusters, saying "poon" for "spoon."

In Spanish, Teo showed difficulty with the tap /ɾ/, often substituting [l] or [d], so "pero" became something closer to "pedo," and he had trouble with the trilled /r/ in words like "perro," which he produced as "peo." These patterns are consistent across both of his languages, which tells me this is a true speech sound disorder rather than a difference tied only to typical second-language learning.

By informal count, Teo's connected speech was only about 3 of 5 words intelligible to an unfamiliar listener, and roughly 8/10 words intelligible to familiar family members, which lines up with what you and Ms. Alvarez have described.

Language Sample and Use

During thirty minutes of play-based observation, I collected a spontaneous language sample in both languages. Teo's utterances were almost all two to three words long, such as "mas jugo" and "doggy run fast," with very few examples of the four- and five-word sentences typical for a child his age. I coded one utterance as [EW] where he appeared to abandon the sentence mid-thought after getting frustrated trying to find the right word.

His mean length of utterance fell in the range expected for a child closer to 2;6–3;0, well below his chronological age of 4;5. He used a good variety of nouns and some verbs, but struggled with grammatical markers in both languages, including plural endings and past tense forms. He did attempt self-correction several times, for example saying "the dog — the doggy runned" before continuing, which shows good self-monitoring skills that we can build on in therapy.

Oral-Motor and Hearing Status

Teo's oral-motor exam was unremarkable; his structures and movement patterns for speech are age-appropriate, and there is no indication of a motor-based speech disorder such as apraxia. Given his history of ear infections and tube placement, and the fact that his classroom hearing screening in March was within normal limits, I recommend a follow-up audiological evaluation only if you or Ms. Alvarez notice any new signs of hearing difficulty.

Impressions

Based on today's testing, parent and teacher report, and classroom observation, Teo meets criteria for a Mixed Receptive-Expressive Language Disorder (ICD-10 F80.2) along with a Speech Sound Disorder (ICD-10 F80.0). Because his error patterns are consistent across both English and Spanish, this appears to be a true disorder affecting both of his languages rather than a difference related to typical bilingual development. His difficulties with intelligibility, sentence length, and grammar are significant enough to affect his ability to be understood by peers and adults outside the family, and are likely contributing to the frustration Ms. Alvarez has observed during circle time.

Recommendations

1. I recommend that Teo begin individual speech-language therapy twice weekly, 30 minutes per session (billed under CPT code 92523 for the initial speech sound evaluation and CPT code 92507 for ongoing treatment sessions), with sessions conducted bilingually given his mixed language exposure at home.

2. I recommend this be written into his IFSP as Outcome 2, targeting intelligibility and expressive language growth, with a review in six months.

3. In therapy, I plan to use a combination of a Cycles approach for his speech sound patterns and PROMPT techniques to support more consistent articulation, along with Hanen-based strategies that you can use at home to expand his sentences during everyday routines.

4. At home, continue exposing Teo to both English and Spanish — there is no need to limit either language. When he says something unclear, try modeling the correct word back to him once, rather than asking him to repeat himself multiple times, which can increase frustration.

5. I recommend Ms. Alvarez continue giving Teo extra processing time during circle time and consider using visual supports when introducing new classroom vocabulary.

6. Please share this report with Ana Gutiérrez and with Dr. Patel so this can be incorporated into Teo's IFSP planning meeting.

7. I recommend a recheck of his progress in approximately four months, around late January, to monitor growth in both intelligibility and sentence length.
