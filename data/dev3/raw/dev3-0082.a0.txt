[[CHILD|Teo]]'s spontaneous speech in Spanish showed substitution patterns consistent with phonological process delay beyond expected age, including fronting of velars (/k/ produced as [t], e.g., "[[KEEP|taza]]" for "casa"), gliding of liquids (/ɾ/ produced as [w]), and final consonant deletion. In English, parallel patterns were noted, with [[CHILD|Teo]] producing "[[KEEP|tat]]" for "cat" and "[[KEEP|boo]]" for "blue," consistent with cross-linguistic phonological transfer rather than two separate disorders. Intelligibility to unfamiliar listeners was estimated at [[KEEP|40%]] in connected speech, improving to [[KEEP|65%]] with context and [[KEEP|mod cues]].

A SALT-coded transcript segment illustrates typical utterance length and error patterns: "[[KEEP|[EW] quiero jugo]]" (I want juice) and "[[KEEP|mas galleta]]" lacking the expected plural/gender agreement marker, consistent with the morphosyntax findings above. Mean length of utterance in morphemes (MLU-m) was calculated at [[KEEP|2.4]] in Spanish and [[KEEP|2.1]] in English, both below expectations for [[KEEP|4;2]].

During the clinic session, [[CHILD|Teo]] was stimulable for target sounds with [[KEEP|min]] to [[KEEP|mod cues]] in both languages, a positive prognostic indicator. He demonstrated strong play skills, symbolic play with trucks and animal figures, and good eye contact and joint attention, though he showed brief frustration-related behaviors (dropping to the floor, pushing materials away) when not understood, consistent with parent and teacher report.

IMPRESSIONS / IMPRESIONES

Based on standardized testing, parent and teacher report, and direct observation, [[CHILD|Teo]] presents with a bilingual language disorder affecting both Spanish and English, characterized by delays in receptive and expressive language, reduced morphosyntactic accuracy, and a co-occurring phonological disorder affecting intelligibility across both languages. Because deficits are present in both of [[CHILD|Teo]]'s languages relative to same-age bilingual peers, these findings reflect a true language disorder rather than typical second-language acquisition or normal bilingual development. Difficulty being understood by unfamiliar communication partners, including classmates such as [[PEER|Ethan Carter]], is contributing to frustration-related behavior at [[DAYCARE|Mud Pie Preschool]] and likely social participation difficulties.

[[CHILD|Teo]] demonstrated good stimulability, strong nonverbal communication foundations, intact hearing, and a supportive, engaged bilingual family, including [[FAMILY|Rosa Jiménez]] and babysitter [[FAMILY|Yolanda Pérez]], all positive prognostic indicators for continued progress with intervention.
